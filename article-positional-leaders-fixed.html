WHY WAR RATES
THESE SEVEN NO. 1

WAR v36  ·  Positional leaders  ·  Round 13

The WAR model compares players within their position group, rewards scarce positions through a positional scarcity multiplier, and accumulates with games played. A key forward is never judged against a general defender. Only against other key forwards. That is what makes the positional leaders worth examining individually. These seven players are not just posting solid numbers. Against every comparable player in the competition, they are the clear standout in their role.

Key Forward  ·  #1

Josh Treacy

Fremantle
2.7
WAR (13 games)

Treacy is not a one-dimensional forward, and that is exactly why the model rates him first at his position through Round 13.

He has kicked 32 goals from 13 games, which is an excellent return, but the goal count alone does not explain where he sits in the rankings. His 2.4 contested marks per game places him at the 100th percentile for key forwards, as does his 92nd-percentile score involvement rate. His 2.4 marks on lead per game sits at the 94th percentile, and his marks inside 50 at the 97th. In other words, he is winning contested ball deep in the forward half, taking marks at the top of the square, getting himself involved in scoring chains and finishing when the opportunity comes.

Key forwards who only take set shots are useful. Key forwards who do everything Treacy does are genuinely rare, and the model is built specifically to reward complete players in their role rather than just the ones who kick the most goals on the weekend.

Gen. Forward  ·  #1

Nick Watson

Hawthorn
2.96
WAR (13 games)

Watson is 21 years old, playing his third full AFL season, and sitting second in the entire competition by accumulated WAR. Most of the conversation around him is still framed as potential. The numbers say it is already reality.

Thirty-three goals from 13 games puts him at the 100th percentile for scoring among general forwards. His ball use equity of 8.7 places him at the top of that category regardless of position. He wins the ball at ground level at the 96th percentile, crumbs at the 91st, and generates a 26.3 percent score involvement rate.

What makes him the clear number one general forward is not dominance in a single category but the fact that he is operating at elite or near-elite level across scoring, contested winning and ball use simultaneously.

Chad Warner

Sydney
2.4

Warner's value comes from doing everything required of a mid-forward at a consistently high level rather than dominating any single category.

He averages 22 disposals, 1.3 goals and 6.8 inside 50s per game, ranking at the 100th percentile for both score involvements and inside 50s, and at the 100th percentile for metres gained and post-clearance equity.

Across 13 consecutive games at that output level, 2.47 accumulated WAR is exactly what you would expect from a player doing this job this well for this long.

Ruck  ·  #1

Luke Jackson

Fremantle
3.15
WAR (13 games)

Jackson is the best ruck in the competition, and the gap between him and the field is meaningful enough that it is not a debate the model entertains for long.

He averages 25.3 hitouts per game with 8.0 directly to advantage, his clearance numbers are genuine at 5.0 per game, and he averages 5.2 tackles. He sits at the 95th percentile among rucks for threat rating, ground ball gets and post-clearance equity simultaneously, and at the 97th for tackles.

Jackson is doing all of it, across 13 consecutive games, and the 3.15 accumulated WAR reflects a player who is simply doing his job better than anyone else in the competition does theirs.

Key Defender  ·  #1

Callum Wilkie

St Kilda
3.21
WAR (13 games)

Wilkie is the third best player in the competition by accumulated WAR, and if you have not been following the numbers closely you would be forgiven for not knowing it.

He averages 8.8 intercepts per game and 3.1 intercept marks, both at the 98th percentile or above for key defenders. He averages 5.5 rebound 50s and generates 622 net metres per game, placing him at the 100th percentile for his position group on that measure.

He is not just preventing scores. He is winning his contest and then moving the ball the length of the ground. That combination of quality and availability produces 3.21 accumulated WAR.

Gen. Defender  ·  #1

Nick Blakey

Sydney
2.69
WAR (13 games)

Blakey is the fourth best player in the competition. Through Round 13 his profile makes the clearest possible case for why a model that looks beyond disposal volume is necessary.

He averages 6.0 intercepts per game, 7.0 rebound 50s and 861 net metres gained, placing him at the 100th percentile among general defenders for rebound 50s. His defensive contest loss percentage is zero.

When Sydney enter their defensive 50, the ball rarely stays there because Blakey takes it and drives it back up. In any defensive structure, a player doing that job at that level is one of the most structurally important assets a team can have.
