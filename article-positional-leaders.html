WHY WAR RATES
THESE SEVEN NO. 1

WAR v36  ·  Positional leaders  ·  Round 13

The WAR model compares players within their position group, rewards scarce positions through a positional scarcity multiplier, and accumulates with games played. A key forward is never judged against a general defender. Only against other key forwards. That is what makes the positional leaders worth examining individually. These seven players are not just posting solid numbers. Against every comparable player in the competition, they are the clear standout in their role.

Key Forward  ·  #1

Josh Treacy

Fremantle
2.7
WAR (13 games)

Treacy is not a one-dimensional forward, and that is exactly why the model rates him first at his position through Round 13.

He has kicked 32 goals from 13 games, which is an excellent return, but the goal count alone does not explain where he sits in the rankings. His 2.4 contested marks per game places him at the 100th percentile for key forwards, as does his 92nd-percentile score involvement rate. His 2.4 marks on lead per game sits at the 94th percentile, and his marks inside 50 at the 97th. In other words, he is winning contested ball deep in the forward half, taking marks at the top of the square, getting himself involved in scoring chains and finishing when the opportunity comes.

Key forwards who only take set shots are useful. Key forwards who do everything Treacy does are genuinely rare, and the model is built specifically to reward complete players in their role rather than just the ones who kick the most goals on the weekend.

Gen. Forward  ·  #1

Nick Watson

Hawthorn
2.96
WAR (13 games)

Watson is 21 years old, playing his third full AFL season, and sitting second in the entire competition by accumulated WAR. Most of the conversation around him is still framed as potential. The numbers say it is already reality.

Thirty-three goals from 13 games puts him at the 100th percentile for scoring among general forwards. His ball use equity of 8.7 places him at the top of that category regardless of position, reflecting how consistently he moves the ball forward rather than sideways. He wins the ball at ground level at the 96th percentile, crumbs at the 91st, and generates a 26.3 percent score involvement rate. He averages 14 disposals per game, and they go forward with purpose.

What makes him the clear number one general forward is not dominance in a single category but the fact that he is operating at elite or near-elite level across scoring, contested winning and ball use simultaneously. Most forwards of this type are strong in one or two of those areas. Watson is strong across all of them, and has been for the entire season.

Isaac Heeney

Sydney
3.33
WAR (11 games)

Heeney is the best player in the competition, and through Round 13 the argument for anyone else requires significant qualification.

Two goals per game, 27 disposals, 5.5 tackles, 6.5 clearances. He is at the 100th percentile among midfielders for scoring, score involvement and threat rating, at the 99th for post-clearance equity and the 98th for contested possessions. There is not a single relevant facet of the midfielder role in which he is not performing at an elite level against every comparable player in the competition.

The model projects him to 6.97 WAR over a full 23-game season, a number that sits in a different bracket from everyone below him on this list. He is not producing these numbers in patches or riding one strong month. Through 11 games he has been consistently dominant across every category the model evaluates, and the 3.33 WAR he has already accumulated represents the most valuable individual contribution to a team in the competition this season.

2.4

Ruck  ·  #1

Luke Jackson

Fremantle
3.15
WAR (13 games)

Jackson is the best ruck in the competition, and the gap between him and the field is meaningful enough that it is not a debate the model entertains for long.

He averages 25.3 hitouts per game with 8.0 directly to advantage, his clearance numbers are genuine at 5.0 per game, and he averages 5.2 tackles. He sits at the 95th percentile among rucks for threat rating, ground ball gets and post-clearance equity simultaneously, and at the 97th for tackles. Those are not numbers that belong to a player simply winning the ruck contest and getting out of the way. They belong to a player who dominates the ruck, wins the clearance and then stays involved.

The ruck position is foundational to team structure in a way that rarely gets acknowledged in public discussion. Controlling hitout ball gives your midfield first use, and a ruck who generates clearances and tackles on top of that is creating compounding value across four quarters. Jackson is doing all of it, across 13 consecutive games, and the 3.15 accumulated WAR reflects a player who is simply doing his job better than anyone else in the competition does theirs.

Key Defender  ·  #1

Callum Wilkie

St Kilda
3.21
WAR (13 games)

Wilkie is the third best player in the competition by accumulated WAR, and if you have not been following the numbers closely you would be forgiven for not knowing it.

He averages 8.8 intercepts per game and 3.1 intercept marks, both at the 98th percentile or above for key defenders. He averages 5.5 rebound 50s and generates 622 net metres per game, placing him at the 100th percentile for his position group on that measure. He has played every game of the season and his defensive contest loss rate sits at 14.6 percent, controlled and disciplined across the full run of games. His score launches rank at the 93rd percentile.

He is not just preventing scores. He is winning his contest, repelling the opposition forward line and then moving the ball the length of the ground. A key defender doing that consistently across all 13 rounds is producing value at both ends of every defensive chain, and 3.21 accumulated WAR is what that combination of quality and availability produces in the model.

Gen. Defender  ·  #1

Nick Blakey

Sydney
2.69
WAR (13 games)

Blakey is the fourth best player in the competition. Through Round 13 his profile makes the clearest possible case for why a model that looks beyond disposal volume is necessary.

He averages 6.0 intercepts per game, 7.0 rebound 50s and 861 net metres gained, placing him at the 100th percentile among general defenders for rebound 50s and the 94th for net metres. His defensive contest loss percentage is zero. He gives up nothing in one-on-one situations, and after winning those contests he moves the ball nearly the length of the ground.

When Sydney enter their defensive 50, the ball rarely stays there because Blakey takes it and drives it back up. He does that every game, in every round, against every forward line. In any defensive structure, a player doing that job at that level is one of the most structurally important assets a team can have, and 2.69 accumulated WAR through 13 games is the number that reflects it.
