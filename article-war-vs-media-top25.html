Isaac Heeney

Heeney's 3.33 WAR is the standout number in the competition, and the distance between him and second place tells a story of its own. He leads his nearest rival by 0.12 wins with two fewer games played, meaning his per-game contribution is even further ahead than the accumulated total suggests. He is averaging career highs across disposals, tackles and goals simultaneously, doing it at the highest post-clearance equity value of any midfielder in the competition, and he has done it consistently across every round he has played. No other player in this dataset is producing across as many categories at the level he is.

Nick Daicos

Daicos is ranked first by the AFL.com.au panel and 51st out of 544 AFL players by WAR, a gap that deserves more than a footnote. The model is not saying he is a bad player. A 1.81 WAR places him as a genuine starter-level performer, the kind of player a club would happily have in their 22. What it is saying is that 47 players have contributed more wins to their team through Round 13, and that ranking first on the media panel while sitting 48th in the model is a significant structural disagreement rather than a marginal one.

The explanation lies in what drives his profile. His 34.9 disposals per game is the number that commands attention, but once the model controls for contested work rate, disposal quality and direct score creation, the output looks more modest than the volume suggests. He accumulates the ball predominantly in space, which the model credits at a lower rate than contested possessions won under pressure. Whether that weighting is correct is a legitimate debate, but the gap between first and 48th is substantial enough that the difference in methodology cannot be dismissed as noise.

Where the model and the panel agree

The convergences are as revealing as the divergences. Nick Watson sits fourth by WAR and 10th by the panel, a gap, but both systems acknowledge he is genuinely elite. Luke Jackson at third by WAR and seventh by the panel is near-perfect alignment between two very different methodologies. Marcus Bontempelli, Bailey Smith and Max Gawn all sit in comparable positions across both lists. The disagreements are not randomly distributed. They cluster specifically around players whose media profile is driven by disposal volume and whose actual contribution to winning situations the model rates more conservatively.

The five players the model says do not belong in the top 25: Daicos (#48), Lachie Neale (#63), Caleb Serong (#126), Noah Anderson (#109), Max Holmes (#46). All producing starter-level WAR. All ranked in the top 25 on reputation or disposal volume.

The players the panel missed entirely

Callum Wilkie ranks second in the competition by WAR and did not appear in the media top 25. Nick Blakey ranks eighth and was similarly absent. Both are defenders generating value through intercepts, rebound 50s and net metres gained rather than through disposal accumulation, which means they tend to be invisible in coverage that equates ball involvement with quality. The panel is not making an irrational choice by favouring midfielders. It is making a systematic one, and the model is designed specifically to correct for it.

Hugh McCluggage

McCluggage ranks 364th in the competition with 0.15 accumulated WAR. AFL.com.au have him 21st. He has averaged 17.2 disposals across 9 games since returning from injury, which is not the output of a top-25 player by any measure the model can produce. The panel is almost certainly rating the player McCluggage was through 2024 and 2025, when his numbers placed him comfortably in elite company. WAR does not carry reputation forward across seasons. It measures what is on the ground in 2026, and what is on the ground in 2026 is a player working his way back to form rather than one producing at the level his panel ranking implies.
