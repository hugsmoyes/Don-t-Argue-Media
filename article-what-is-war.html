WAR · Explainer

WHAT IS WAR?

How we measure player value in AFL football

WAR stands for Wins Above Replacement. The concept comes from baseball, where it has been used for decades to answer a simple question: how many wins does this player add to their team compared to a replacement level player, someone you could pick up off the waiver wire?

In AFL, we have adapted the framework to work with Champion Data's player-level stats. The goal is the same. A single number that captures a player's total contribution and makes them directly comparable regardless of position or role.

Why WAR?

Football has always had statistics. What it has lacked is a single number that tells you how much a player is actually worth. Disposals tell you how often someone touches the ball. Goals tell you how often a forward scores. Neither tells you whether the player made their team better. WAR is an attempt to answer that question properly.

The model goes beyond face value. A player who accumulates disposals in space is treated differently from one who wins the ball in contested situations and moves it efficiently forward. A defender who generates eight intercepts and repels a forward line is valued for that work in a way that counting stats simply cannot capture. The model is looking for impact, not just activity.

The result rewards complete players. The players who rank highest in WAR are not always the ones who dominate the highlight reel or the disposal tally. They are the ones who contribute across every relevant category in their role, do it consistently, do it for long stretches of the season, and do it in ways that directly influence their team's ability to win football games. That is the number we are trying to produce.

The 21 stats

WAR uses 21 per-game stats. They cover five categories: offensive output including goals, score involvements, threat rating, expected score metrics and contested marks; transition work including I50 kick retention, score launches, net metres gained, ball use equity and retained disposals; defensive impact including intercepts, intercept marks, rebound 50s and one percenters; contest work including hard ball gets, stoppage clearances, tackles and hitouts to advantage; and two negative stats that penalise turnovers and defensive contest losses.

The weighting matters as much as the selection. Goals are weighted at 3.5 times their z-score because a direct scoring act is the highest-value event in the game. Intercepts and intercept marks are each weighted at 1.5, reflecting how directly they change defensive situations. Tackles and one percenters sit at 1.0. The result is a composite that rewards the events that most strongly predict winning, rather than treating a goal and a handball as equivalent contributions because both show up in the disposal count.

Three of the 21 stats are derived rather than directly measured. Retained disposals multiplies a player's disposal count by their disposal retention rate, so that a player who gets 25 disposals and keeps 78 percent of them rates higher than one who gets 25 disposals and keeps 58 percent. Adjusted clangers removes the clangers that result from legitimate contested attempts, because forcing the issue in a hard contest and turning it over is a different act from a lazy kick that goes straight to the opposition. Defensive contests lost estimates how much value a player concedes in direct one-on-one defensive situations.

The calculation pipeline

Step 1. Each of the 21 stats is converted to a season-average per-game figure, then z-scored against a frozen 2025 reference population — the 617 players who featured in at least three games last season — with values capped at plus or minus 3.0. That population never changes. A player's number this week is measured against last year's competition, not against whoever else happens to be in form right now.

Step 2. Each z-scored stat is multiplied by its category weight. Goals are worth 3.5 times their z-score. Intercepts and intercept marks are worth 1.5. Tackles and one percenters sit at 1.0. The weights reflect how strongly each stat predicts winning.

Step 3. The weighted stats are summed into a single composite score per player per game.

Step 4. The composite is z-scored a second time, this time within the player's position group — and again against the frozen 2025 population. A key forward's composite is measured against the mean and spread of key forwards from 2025. A ruck is measured against 2025 rucks. This is the step that makes WAR position-neutral: a key defender racking up intercepts is judged against other key defenders, not against a midfielder racking up disposals.

Step 5. A fixed replacement baseline is subtracted from that within-position z-score — the 25th percentile for that position, again taken from the 2025 reference population. What's left is a raw value-above-replacement number for that game. A player whose underlying output matches the 2025 25th percentile for their position scores zero here, this year or any year, regardless of who else is playing well.

Step 6. That raw number is multiplied by a calibration constant (≈0.0653) and then by games played to produce WAR. This is where WAR becomes accumulative. A player sitting above replacement adds to their total every week they take the field. A player below replacement loses ground every week. Because every constant in the pipeline is fixed to the 2025 baseline, that addition or subtraction never gets recalculated retroactively — what a player banked in round 4 stays banked no matter what happens in round 14.

WAR as an accumulative stat

WAR accumulates with games played, and the frozen baseline is what makes that meaningful rather than a moving target. Because the z-score means, standard deviations and replacement thresholds are all locked to the 2025 season, "replacement level" is a fixed line — not a description of how this year's competition happens to be performing. A player whose output sits exactly on that 2025 25th-percentile line for their position scores a raw value of zero and adds nothing to their WAR total no matter how many games they play. A player clearly above it adds to their total every week. A player clearly below it loses ground every week. The line itself does not move.

That fixed ruler is also why bye rounds and DNPs no longer move the numbers. WAR depends only on a player's own season averages, their own games played, and the frozen 2025 constants — nothing about the live competition feeds into the calculation. A player on a bye, sitting out injured, or watching from the bench banks the same WAR total this week as last week. Under the old model, a quiet week for the competition as a whole could nudge everyone's number up or down even if a player didn't take the field. That can't happen now.

It also means totals are directly comparable across the season. A player averaging 0.25 WAR per game across all 23 rounds finishes on roughly 5.75 — not because the model annualises performance, but because that's literally 23 lots of 0.25 added up game by game. A player who starts the year out of form and finds it in the second half will show that arc in the shape of their cumulative total, not in a single retroactive jump. The number on the board after round 14 is the number on the board after round 14, full stop — it won't be different if you check again after round 20.

What WAR does not measure

WAR is a statistical model. It works from the numbers Champion Data records. It does not see leadership, pressure under finals conditions, the impact of a player on team culture or the strategic value of a player's movement that does not show up in box-score events. It also does not adjust for role within a team structure. A player asked to play a limiting role will produce lower WAR than a player given freedom to roam, even if the limiting role is the more important one for the team.

These are real limitations. The model is a starting point, not a verdict. But it is a more rigorous starting point than disposal counts and a more honest one than reputation.
