WHAT IS WAR?

By Hugo Moyes June 2026 Updated each round

Every week, AFL fans argue about the best players in the competition. That argument almost always comes down to feel. Who's been dominant lately, who you'd build a team around. These are reasonable instincts. They're also almost impossible to settle, because everyone's watching different things.

WAR, Wins Above Replacement, is an attempt to put a single comparable number against every player's season. How many wins did they add above what a replacement-level player would have provided in the same role? It's one number, reproducible and transparent, and it doesn't care about your priors.

WHERE WAR COMES FROM

Baseball invented it. The core idea is simple: compare any player to a freely available "replacement", the kind of fringe player you'd call up if someone got injured, and measure how many wins the better player is worth over that baseline. Zero means you're replacement level. Five or six in a season and you're an MVP candidate. Ten-plus and you're Mike Trout.

Basketball borrowed the concept and called it VORP, Value Over Replacement Player. FiveThirtyEight built a public version called RAPTOR using tracking data. ESPN's RPM tracks it in real time. In both sports WAR-style metrics have gone from niche analytics content to mainstream broadcast conversation. The goal at Don't Argue Media is to build the same literacy for AFL.

HOW THE MODEL WORKS

The AFL version uses 21 per-game statistics pulled from Wheelo Ratings, grouped across five areas of contribution.

Offensive
Goals · Score Involvements · Score Involvement % · Inside 50s · Tackles Inside 50 · Kicks · Disposal Efficiency
Transition
Rebound 50s · Intercepts · Handballs · Total Disposals
Defensive
Marks · Contested Marks · Rebound 50s (weighted separately) · Frees Differential
Contested
Contested Possessions · Contested Possession Rate · Total Clearances · Hitouts (ruck-adjusted)
Negative
Frees Against · Turnovers (derived from disposal efficiency and kick percentage)

From there, three things happen. First, an opponent-quality adjustment is applied. A game against Collingwood's defensive structure is weighted higher than the same numbers against a bottom-four side. Second, every player's stats are converted to z-scores, measuring how many standard deviations above or below the mean they sit. This is done entirely within their position group. A key forward is measured against key forwards. A ruck against rucks. This is the step that separates the model from a simple leaderboard. Third, those z-scores are weighted by how much each stat has historically contributed to winning AFL games, and converted into an estimated wins figure. That's your WAR.

The model is currently on version 35. The version number reflects significant changes to the weighting methodology or position classification, not just a data refresh after each round. The data itself updates after every game.

WHAT THE MODEL CAN'T DO

There are real limitations here and they're worth being clear about.

It can't watch games. WAR sees statistics. It doesn't know that a player is the reason their teammates are getting the ball, or that a defender is making life impossible for their opponent without ever touching it. Statistics are a shadow of performance.

Injury time is a problem. WAR is a season accumulation metric. Miss eight games and your total tanks, even if you're elite when available. Matt Rowell's 0.84 WAR from six games is a good example. The model understands nothing about being unavailable, only about what happened on the field. A per-game WAR figure is planned to address this.

Position classification isn't perfect. AFL is a fluid sport. Luke Jackson plays ruck, midfielder and forward in the same game. The model assigns a primary position and compares him there, which may not fully reflect how elite he is across all three roles.

Defence is hard to measure. Every public analytics model across every sport undersells defenders. The model uses intercepts, rebound 50s and contested marks as proxies, but a key defender who nullifies their opponent generates value the numbers don't fully capture. The best defenders in this competition are probably underrated by WAR.

What v35 means. The model has gone through 35 methodology revisions since its first version, refining the weighting, opponent adjustment and position classification each time. The core architecture (21 stats, position z-scoring, wins conversion) has been consistent since v12.

WHY BOTHER?

The point isn't to replace your eye test. It's to give it something to push back against. When the model agrees with the consensus (Daicos is elite, Heeney is excellent) that's reinforcement. When it disagrees sharply, like placing Brodie Grundy 16 spots higher than the AFL.com.au panel. That's a question worth asking: is the model wrong, or is the panel underweighting hitout dominance?

Working through that gap is where the interesting analysis lives. The model is a starting point, not an ending one.
